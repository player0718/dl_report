\paragraph{Results.}
As shown in \cref{tab:wd}, weight decay gives a modest but consistent gain
under the 100-epoch protocol. The no-decay baseline reaches $81.90\%$ best
validation accuracy and $81.81\%$ test accuracy, while the best setting,
$\lambda=5\times10^{-2}$, improves these values to $82.56\%$ and
$82.23\%$, respectively, with the smallest 100-epoch train--val gap
($11.02$ pp). The neighboring settings $\lambda=10^{-4}$ and
$\lambda=10^{-2}$ perform similarly, reaching $82.21\%$ and $82.17\%$ test
accuracy, whereas the strongest coefficient, $\lambda=10^{-1}$, drops to
$81.25\%$. In the 30-epoch reference runs, the best test accuracy is
obtained by $\lambda=10^{-2}$ ($84.74\%$), while $\lambda=10^{-1}$ gives
the highest validation accuracy ($84.56\%$); all 30-epoch gaps remain much
smaller than those observed after 100 epochs.

\paragraph{Discussion.}
These results suggest that AdamW weight decay is helpful, but it is not a
sufficient regularizer by itself. The 100-epoch learning curves in
\cref{fig:wd-curves,fig:wd-gap} show that the longer schedule allows the
model to keep fitting the training set, so all settings eventually develop
an approximately 11-point train--validation gap. Moderate decay therefore
acts mainly as a capacity constraint: $\lambda=5\times10^{-2}$ provides the
best balance between suppressing overfitting and preserving useful
representations, while $\lambda=10^{-1}$ begins to reduce model capacity.
The 30-epoch results should be interpreted as a short-horizon comparison
rather than a direct contradiction, because changing the epoch budget also
changes the cosine learning-rate trajectory. Overall, weight decay provides
a small improvement in the longer fine-tuning regime, but larger
generalization gains require stronger regularization mechanisms.
